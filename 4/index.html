01

Photographing
for Overlap

I photographed three scenes in overlapping pairs, rotating the camera from approximately one viewpoint instead of translating it. Each pair preserves enough shared structure to identify the same physical landmarks in both images.

The bedroom is dominated by a rigid bed frame, the bicycle area mixes several depths, and the kitchen offers long horizontal cabinet and counter edges. Together they expose both the strengths and limitations of a single planar projective model.

Bedroom

Repeated frame intersections and drawer corners provide stable landmarks throughout the overlap.

Bicycle alcove

Wall seams and fixed structural details anchor the alignment; nearby bicycles reveal foreground parallax.

Kitchen

Cabinet seams, the counter edge, and fixed appliance features distribute correspondences across the shared view.

02

Recovering
Homographies

A homography maps a source point to a destination point in homogeneous coordinates. Fixing h33 = 1 leaves eight unknowns. Every correspondence contributes two linear equations, so eight matches form an overdetermined system that I solve with NumPy least squares.

Before solving, I apply Hartley normalization: translate each point set to zero mean and scale its average distance from the origin to √2. I then denormalize the fitted matrix. This improves conditioning without changing the projective mapping.

p′ ∼ Hp

The final coordinate is obtained by dividing the first two homogeneous components by the third. In each mosaic, H maps the second photograph into the unchanged reference photograph.

xh11 + yh12 + h13 - ux h31 - uy h32 = u xh21 + yh22 + h23 - vx h31 - vy h32 = v

Stacking these two rows for every (x, y) → (u, v) match gives Ah = b. Well-spread, non-collinear points make the fit substantially more stable.

03

Inverse Warping
& Rectification

Forward mapping can leave holes because transformed source pixels do not necessarily land on integer destination coordinates. Instead, I enumerate every destination pixel, apply H-1, and sample the resulting floating-point location in the source image.

For rectification, four photographed corners map to a rectangle with the physical surface's aspect ratio. The laptop result is especially revealing: after rectification, text captured at an angle becomes directly readable.

psource ∼ H-1pdestination

A validity mask rejects samples outside the source image. Nearest-neighbor rounds to one pixel; bilinear interpolation blends the surrounding four pixels using their fractional x and y distances.

Nearest neighbor

Preserves exact source values, but diagonal edges and small text can look jagged.

Bilinear

Uses four weighted samples for smoother edges, with a small amount of interpolation blur.

Validity mask

Keeps out-of-bounds samples from contributing to either rectified images or mosaics.

04

Feather-Blended
Mosaics

I keep the reference image in its original coordinate system, project the second image into that system, and compute a canvas large enough to contain both. In their overlap, each image receives a weight equal to its distance from the nearest validity-mask boundary; normalizing the two weights creates a gradual transition instead of a hard seam.

The angled outer boundaries are the honest footprint of projective warping. Black regions are uncovered canvas, not missing image data.

I = (waIa + wbIb) / (wa + wb)

Distance-transform weights are largest inside each image and taper toward its boundary. The blend softens exposure differences while preserving all valid pixels covered by either view.

Part B

From clicks
to features

Part A established the geometry; Part B removes the manual point picker. Following the simplified MOPS pipeline, each image now proposes its own corners, describes them locally, matches them across the pair, and lets RANSAC decide which correspondences agree on one projective model.

I reused the same three photo pairs and the same inverse warp and feathering code. That isolates the real experiment: changing only how the homography correspondences are obtained.

05

Harris Corners
& ANMS

I begin with the provided single-scale Harris detector and retain local response maxima outside a 24-pixel border. This produces tens of thousands of candidates—dense around texture, but too clustered to form a stable, efficient matching set.

Adaptive Non-Maximal Suppression assigns each candidate the distance to its nearest substantially stronger neighbor. Keeping the 500 largest radii spreads points across the full frame while preserving locally distinctive structure. I use crobust = 0.9.

ri = min ||xi - xj||

The minimum considers points with f(xj) > f(xi) / 0.9. Large radii identify strong corners that are not crowded by an even stronger response.

06

Local Appearance
in 64 Numbers

Around every ANMS point, I extract an axis-aligned 40×40 grayscale window. After a Gaussian blur, averaging each 5×5 cell reduces the neighborhood to an 8×8 descriptor. Sampling a broad window makes the representation less sensitive to one-pixel shifts than an unblurred 8×8 crop.

Each 64-value vector is bias/gain normalized to zero mean and unit standard deviation. The descriptor therefore emphasizes the local intensity pattern instead of absolute exposure.

d = (p - μp) / σp

The course simplification uses axis-aligned descriptors, so this stage is not rotation invariant. Every retained point still has a complete 40×40 support window because corners near the image boundary were removed first.

07

Matching by
Distinctiveness

I compute every pairwise descriptor distance. A proposed match survives when its closest neighbor is much better than its second-closest neighbor: d1 / d2 < 0.80. This is Lowe's ratio test; it rejects ambiguous patches such as repeated cabinet or bed-frame edges.

I also require mutual nearest neighbors. The remaining lines are plausible appearance matches, not yet geometric truth—several outliers are still visible, which is exactly what RANSAC must handle next.

08

Four Points,
Five Thousand Times

My RANSAC implementation repeatedly samples four non-degenerate matches, fits a homography with the Part A solver, and measures every tentative match's reprojection error. A match is an inlier when its projected point lands within 5 pixels of the observed destination.

After 5,000 trials, I keep the largest consensus set and recompute the homography from all of its inliers using normalized least squares. This final refit is much less sensitive to any one correspondence than the four-point proposal that discovered the set.

1

Sample four matches without replacement.

2

Reject nearly collinear samples and solve for H.

3

Count reprojection errors at or below 5 px.

4

Refit H using every inlier in the best consensus.

10

What I
Learned

Clicks become geometry

A few pixels of correspondence error can noticeably move an entire projected image.

Invert the mapping

Asking where every destination pixel came from avoids the holes created by forward splatting.

Masks make blending honest

Distance weights work only when invalid warped pixels are excluded explicitly.

Parallax is not a bad homography

One projective transform cannot perfectly align a translated camera view containing many depths.

Strong is not enough

ANMS turns a pile of strong corners into a geometrically useful distribution across the image.

Context beats a pixel

Blurred 40×40 neighborhoods remain recognizable after small shifts that would break tiny raw patches.

Ambiguity is measurable

The nearest-neighbor ratio exposes when a descriptor has no clearly distinctive partner.

Consensus beats perfection

RANSAC succeeds without requiring every proposed match to be correct.
